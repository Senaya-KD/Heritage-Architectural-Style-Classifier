# Conservation Guidance for Heritage Sites

## General Principles

Historic buildings are conserved according to the principle of **minimum
intervention**: repairing only what is necessary to keep a structure safe
and weathertight, using materials and methods compatible with the
original construction, rather than replacing historic fabric
unnecessarily. Every original material lost during "restoration" is
permanently lost evidence of how the building was actually built.

## Common Issues by Style

**Romanesque and Gothic (stone-built, often ecclesiastical)**
Principal risks are stonework decay from weathering and pollution,
structural movement in tall vaulted structures, and water ingress through
aging roofs and gutters. Stonework is typically monitored for erosion and
repaired using matching stone types rather than modern substitutes, which
can react badly with historic masonry.

**Georgian and Baroque (brick and stone, urban)**
Common issues include damp caused by inappropriate modern materials
(such as cement render, which traps moisture in ways lime-based
historic materials did not), and structural stress from converting
buildings to modern uses.

**Tudor Revival (timber and brick, domestic)**
Where original timber framing survives, the main risks are wood decay
and pest damage. In Revival buildings, where the timber is decorative
rather than structural, conservation is generally more about maintaining
appearance and weatherproofing behind the timber facing.

**Art Deco and Art Nouveau (early 20th century, mixed materials)**
These buildings often use early reinforced concrete, glazed tile, and
decorative ironwork, all of which have their own specific decay patterns
—reinforcement corrosion in concrete, and cracking or loss of glazed
finishes—that are less familiar to conservators trained mainly on older
masonry buildings.

## Visitor Impact

Heritage sites face pressure from footfall, touch, and photography
(particularly flash photography, which can damage sensitive pigments and
textiles over time). Visitors should stay on marked routes, avoid
touching decorative stonework, plaster, or painted surfaces, and follow
any photography restrictions in place at a given site.

## Why This Matters for This System

An AI classification and explanation system supporting a heritage trust
should be explicit about what it does and does not know regarding a
building's condition. Style classification identifies architectural
period and form; it says nothing about structural safety or conservation
status. Any structural concern must be reported to trust staff directly,
never inferred from an AI-generated response.
