# Art Deco Architecture: History and Context

## Period and Origins

Art Deco emerged in France in the early 1920s, taking its name — itself a
later abbreviation coined by historians — from the 1925 Exposition
Internationale des Arts Décoratifs et Industriels Modernes held in Paris,
an international exhibition specifically dedicated to showcasing modern
decorative and industrial design. The style spread rapidly across Western
Europe and the United States over the following decade, becoming one of
the single most immediately recognisable and widely applied visual
styles of the entire interwar period.

The movement was significantly shaped and reinforced by the widely
publicised discovery of Tutankhamun's tomb in Egypt in 1922, which
sparked a genuine wave of popular public interest in ancient Egyptian
visual motifs — bold geometric patterning, strong symmetry, and stylised
natural forms — all of which fed directly and visibly into Art Deco's
developing decorative vocabulary throughout the 1920s.

## From Luxury Objects to Everyday Buildings

Art Deco began principally as a style applied to luxury consumer goods
and private interiors, but it expanded rapidly into full architectural
practice, where it was applied across a very wide range of public and
commercial building types — cinemas, department stores, hotels, and,
particularly distinctively in Britain, factories. Its bold geometric
appearance and strong cultural association with modern glamour made it a
favoured choice for buildings that specifically wanted to project a sense
of confident, forward-looking, technologically modern identity to the
public.

## Art Deco in Britain

In England, Art Deco is strongly and specifically associated with the
rapid growth of new light industries built along major arterial roads
during the 1930s. The **Hoover Building** in Ealing, west London,
completed in 1933 for the British arm of the American Hoover Company, is
one of the most celebrated architectural examples anywhere in the
country — a factory building treated with exactly the same level of
decorative architectural ambition normally reserved for prestigious civic
buildings. Other notable English examples include the richly decorated
Art Deco interior of the Strand Palace Hotel in London and the striking
fountain staircase within the Shakespeare Memorial Theatre, now known as
the Royal Shakespeare Theatre, in Stratford-upon-Avon.

Cinemas were a particularly important building type for British Art Deco
specifically, since the extremely rapid growth of the British cinema
industry during the 1930s coincided almost exactly with the style's
period of greatest national popularity, producing hundreds of Art Deco
"picture palaces" across the country, many of which survive today,
though often converted to other uses.

## Art Deco in Scotland

Scotland produced its own significant body of Art Deco work, particularly
associated with the 1938 Empire Exhibition held in Glasgow's Bellahouston
Park, which showcased contemporary Scottish and international Art Deco
and Streamline Moderne design on a large temporary scale, and with
several surviving Art Deco cinemas and commercial buildings across
Glasgow and Edinburgh from the same decade.

## Relationship to Art Nouveau

Art Deco is frequently described by historians as a direct reaction
against the earlier Art Nouveau movement, which had flourished roughly
between 1890 and 1910. Where Art Nouveau favoured flowing, organic,
plant-inspired curves throughout, Art Deco deliberately replaced these
with sharp, geometric, distinctly machine-age forms instead — reflecting
a much broader cultural shift toward celebrating industry, speed, and
modern technology, rather than the natural world that had inspired the
previous generation of designers. Despite this clear stylistic contrast,
the two movements share a strong underlying emphasis on rich decorative
surface treatment and applied ornament, which is part of why they
continue to be confused with one another by non-specialists.

## Relevance to UK Heritage Sites

A significant number of English and Scottish Art Deco buildings are now
formally listed heritage assets, valued both for their striking
architectural distinctiveness in their own right and as important
physical evidence documenting Britain's interwar industrial, commercial,
and leisure expansion during a pivotal decade of national economic
change.
