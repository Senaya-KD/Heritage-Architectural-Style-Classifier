# Art Nouveau Architecture: History and Context

## Period and Origins

Art Nouveau, meaning simply "new art" in French, emerged in the early
1890s and flourished for a relatively short but architecturally
influential period until around 1910, making it one of the shorter-lived
major architectural movements of the modern era, even though its
influence on decorative design lasted considerably longer. The style
developed almost simultaneously across several major European cities,
taking on a slightly different name and a distinct local character in
each: **Jugendstil** in Germany, **Sezessionstil** associated with the
Vienna Secession in Austria, **Modernisme** in Catalonia, associated
above all with the singular and extraordinary work of Antoni Gaudí in
Barcelona, and Art Nouveau proper in France and Belgium, where the
movement first took clear architectural form.

The style developed, at least in part, as a deliberate and self-conscious
reaction against the historical revivalism that had dominated so much of
19th-century European architecture — the Gothic Revival, the various
competing classical revival movements, and so on. Art Nouveau architects
explicitly wanted to create a genuinely new visual language for their own
time, one that did not simply recycle and rearrange the forms of the
past, and they found their principal inspiration instead in the flowing,
organic forms of the natural world — plant stems, flower forms, insect
wings, and flowing water and hair.

## Key Figures

**Victor Horta** in Brussels and **Hector Guimard** in Paris — designer
of the now-iconic entrance canopies for the Paris Métro system — were
among the style's most architecturally influential practitioners,
alongside **Antoni Gaudí**, whose highly individual and instantly
recognisable work in Barcelona, including the still-unfinished Sagrada
Família basilica and the extraordinary Casa Batlló, represents one of the
most extreme, celebrated, and internationally famous expressions of Art
Nouveau architectural form anywhere in the world.

## Art Nouveau in Britain

Art Nouveau had a somewhat more limited direct architectural presence in
Britain than it achieved on the European continent, but it found a
genuinely distinctive and internationally influential British voice
through the work of **Charles Rennie Mackintosh** in Glasgow, whose
designs for the **Glasgow School of Art**, built between 1897 and 1909,
combine Art Nouveau's characteristic organic decorative sensibility with
a noticeably more restrained, geometric, and distinctly Scottish
architectural character of his own devising — a synthesis that would
itself go on to influence the wider European Art Nouveau movement, rather
than simply following continental precedent. Mackintosh's associated
circle, sometimes called "the Glasgow Four," extended this distinctive
Scottish interpretation of Art Nouveau across furniture, interior
design, and graphic art as well as architecture proper.

British Art Nouveau also flourished strongly and independently within the
applied and decorative arts more broadly — glassware, textiles, jewellery,
and graphic design in particular — even in places where a fully realised
architectural expression of the style remained comparatively less common
than it was in France or Belgium during the same period. The department
store Liberty & Co. in London became closely associated internationally
with the style, to the extent that Art Nouveau is still sometimes referred
to in Italy as "stile Liberty" in direct reference to the London shop.

## Decline

Art Nouveau's widespread popularity faded quite quickly after around
1910, for two related reasons: its highly individual, extremely
labour-intensive decorative work was expensive to produce and genuinely
difficult to mass-produce at any scale, and changing public taste was
already moving toward the more geometric, confidently machine-age
sensibility that would very shortly crystallise into the Art Deco
movement during the following decade.

## Relevance to UK Heritage Sites

While genuinely rarer in Britain than on the European continent,
surviving British Art Nouveau buildings — the Glasgow School of Art
foremost among them — are of exceptional heritage significance
specifically because of their relative scarcity, and their ongoing
conservation has attracted major national and international attention,
particularly following serious fire damage suffered by the Glasgow
School of Art in both 2014 and again in 2018, which prompted a
substantial and closely watched international conservation and
restoration effort.
