# Baroque Architecture: History and Context

## Period and Origins

Baroque architecture originated in Italy during the late 16th and early
17th centuries, closely tied historically to the Catholic Church's
deliberate response to the Protestant Reformation — a movement art
historians generally refer to as the Counter-Reformation. The Church
specifically wanted new buildings that would overwhelm and emotionally
inspire worshippers through sheer drama, scale, and sensory intensity, in
deliberate and calculated contrast to the plainer, more austere worship
spaces generally favoured by Protestant reformers across northern Europe.
The style subsequently spread rapidly across Catholic Europe and, in a
notably more restrained and adapted form, eventually reached
predominantly Protestant Britain as well.

## English Baroque

**English Baroque** developed somewhat later than its Italian and French
counterparts and in a notably more restrained, less exuberant form,
running roughly from the 1660s through to the 1720s — corresponding to
the late Stuart period of English history. Its two dominant architectural
figures were Sir Christopher Wren and Sir John Vanbrugh, the latter
working closely alongside the architect Nicholas Hawksmoor on several
major commissions.

Wren's rebuilding of **St Paul's Cathedral**, undertaken following the
building's near-total destruction in the Great Fire of London in 1666, is
the single most significant Baroque building anywhere in England. Wren
additionally designed fifty-one further City of London parish churches in
the decades that followed the fire, each with its own inventive spire
design, collectively giving the City of London its distinctive Baroque
skyline of domes and spires that survived largely intact until the
Blitz. Vanbrugh, working in close partnership with Hawksmoor, went on to
produce grander, more dramatic English country houses, most famously
**Blenheim Palace** in Oxfordshire, built between 1705 and 1722 as a
gift to the Duke of Marlborough in recognition of his military victories.

## Scottish Baroque

Scotland developed its own significant Baroque tradition somewhat in
parallel with events in England, closely associated with the architect
**William Adam**, father of the more famous Robert Adam. William Adam's
**Hopetoun House**, near Edinburgh, remodelled and greatly extended from
1721 onward, stands as one of the finest and most complete examples of
Scottish Baroque country house architecture, combining dramatic massing
and rich decorative detail very much in keeping with the wider European
Baroque tradition of the period.

## Decline

English Baroque fell rapidly and decisively out of fashion after 1714,
the year the Hanoverian dynasty took the British throne. The style's
strong prior association with the recently deposed Stuart monarchs, and
its perceived stylistic closeness to Catholic continental taste, made it
politically as well as aesthetically unfashionable under the new
Protestant regime. Architects and their wealthy patrons turned instead,
quite quickly, to the calmer, more classically "correct" Palladian style,
which went on to form the direct architectural foundation of Georgian
architecture — the style that succeeded Baroque as Britain's dominant
mode almost immediately.

## Relevance to UK Heritage Sites

Baroque buildings in Britain are relatively few in overall number
compared with the very large body of surviving Gothic or Georgian work,
but they include some of the country's single most architecturally and
historically significant structures, including St Paul's Cathedral itself
and several of London's most visually distinctive surviving parish
churches. Their considerable scale and structural complexity — large
domes, elaborate carved stonework, deliberately dramatic massing — present
particular and ongoing conservation demands centred on structural
monitoring of large masonry domes and the specialist repair of highly
decorative external stone carving.
