# Georgian Architecture: What to Look For

## The Defining Feature: Symmetry

Georgian buildings are organised around strict, almost mathematically
precise symmetry. The front door sits at the exact centre of the facade,
with windows arranged in identical, evenly spaced rows and columns
extending equally to either side of it. If you can draw a single vertical
line down the centre of a building and find that the two halves mirror
each other closely, you are very likely looking at genuine Georgian work.
This principle extends through the whole building: chimneys, roof pitch,
and window proportions are all typically matched on either side of the
central axis.

## Proportion Over Ornament

Unlike the heavily decorated Baroque style it directly succeeded, Georgian
architecture favours deliberate restraint. Facades are frequently plain
brick or smooth, undecorated stone, with ornament kept simple and
concentrated specifically around key focal points — most often the front
door — rather than spread liberally across the whole building surface.
Georgian architects and their patrons believed, following Palladian
theory, that correct mathematical proportion, not applied surface
decoration, was what genuinely made a building beautiful.

## Windows

Georgian windows are themselves a strong and distinctive identifying
feature: tall, multi-paned **sash windows**, which slide vertically within
their frame rather than opening outward on hinges, are almost universal.
These are usually arranged with noticeably larger window openings on the
ground floor and progressively smaller windows on each successive floor
above, a visual device that directly reflects the declining social and
practical importance of the upper storeys. A pattern of six small panes
over six small panes per window sash is extremely common throughout the
Georgian period, though later Georgian and Regency windows sometimes use
fewer, larger panes as glass manufacturing technology improved.

## The Front Door

Georgian front doors are frequently marked by a **pediment** — a
triangular or gently curved decorative element positioned directly above
the doorway — sometimes supported on a pair of classical columns or
flattened decorative pilasters attached to the wall surface. A
semicircular, fan-shaped window positioned directly above the door,
called a **fanlight**, is another extremely common Georgian feature,
allowing useful daylight into what would otherwise be a dark entrance
hall.

## Materials and Colour

Brick is by far the most common Georgian building material in England,
typically a warm red-brown or buff colour, frequently combined with
lighter-coloured stone "dressings" used selectively around windows,
doorways, and building corners for visual definition. Terraces were very
often built using a uniform material and consistent colour palette across
an entire street or square specifically to reinforce the sense of a
single, unified architectural composition rather than a row of
individually designed buildings.

## Roofline

Georgian roofs are typically low-pitched and are very often deliberately
hidden from street level entirely, concealed behind a plain parapet wall,
keeping visual emphasis firmly on the flat, ordered, symmetrical facade
below rather than drawing any attention to the roof shape above.

## Quick Identification Checklist

- Strict overall facade symmetry, with a centred front door
- Tall sash windows, typically multi-paned, decreasing in size floor by
  floor
- Restrained, materially uniform brick or stone facade
- Decoration concentrated specifically around the doorway — pediment,
  columns, fanlight
- Low-pitched roof, frequently hidden entirely behind a parapet
- Buildings arranged as part of a coordinated terrace, crescent, or square

## Common Confusion

Georgian architecture is most often confused with **Baroque**, the style
it directly succeeded in Britain — both are ultimately classical in
inspiration and share a common architectural vocabulary of columns and
pediments, but Baroque uses far more dramatic decoration, curved or
undulating forms, and dynamic overall massing, while Georgian remains
deliberately calmer, flatter, and considerably more restrained throughout.
Georgian architecture is also occasionally confused with older buildings
that were remodelled with a new, symmetrical Georgian-style facade during
the 18th century, a very common contemporary practice when older
buildings, including genuine Tudor-period structures, were "modernised"
by simply refacing their street frontage.
