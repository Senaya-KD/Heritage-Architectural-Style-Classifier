# Romanesque Architecture: What to Look For

## The Defining Feature: The Round Arch

If you remember one thing about Romanesque architecture, remember this:
the arches are **semicircular**, forming a perfect half-circle from one
supporting point to the other. This is the single clearest and most
reliable way to distinguish Romanesque work from Gothic, which uses a
pointed arch instead. Look at any doorway, window, or arcade opening — if
the top curves in one continuous, unbroken round sweep rather than
meeting at a point, you are looking at Romanesque construction. This test
works even when you know nothing else about a building, and it is the
fastest identification shortcut available for this style.

## Walls and Mass

Romanesque buildings look and feel deliberately heavy. Walls are thick —
frequently several feet of solid masonry, sometimes filled with rubble
core between outer stone faces — because the round arch pushes a
significant portion of its structural load outward as well as downward,
and without external buttressing, only sheer mass can safely resist that
outward thrust. This gives Romanesque buildings a squat, fortress-like
silhouette, standing in sharp contrast to the soaring verticality that
Gothic architecture would later achieve using thinner walls and external
support.

## Windows

Because thick walls could only safely support relatively small openings,
Romanesque windows are narrow and set deep within the wall thickness,
often widening dramatically on the interior face (an arrangement called a
**splayed opening**) in order to let in as much usable light as possible
from what is, externally, a modest opening. Windows are almost always
round-topped, matching the arch form used consistently throughout the
building.

## Towers and Silhouette

Romanesque church towers are typically squat and block-like, with small,
widely spaced window openings and a solid, weighty presence, quite unlike
the tall, pierced, delicately traceried towers that Gothic cathedrals
would later produce. Roof lines are generally simple and low-pitched, and
towers frequently sit at the crossing point of nave and transepts rather
than soaring above a west front.

## Decoration

Ornament in Romanesque architecture is geometric and is carved directly
into the stone itself rather than applied afterward as a separate
decorative layer. Common recurring motifs include:

- **Chevron or zigzag patterning**, carved in continuous bands around
  arches and doorways — one of the single most recognisable Romanesque
  decorative devices, seen extensively at Durham Cathedral
- **Interlaced blind arcading** — rows of small, purely decorative arches
  carved or built into a flat wall surface, used to break up otherwise
  plain expanses of masonry
- Simple **cushion capitals** atop columns, typically a plain cubic block
  shape with the lower corners rounded off into a curve, far less
  elaborate than the foliage-carved capitals of the later Gothic period
- Carved **tympana** — the semicircular panel above a doorway, frequently
  decorated with relief carving of religious scenes, animals, or symbolic
  figures

## Quick Identification Checklist

- Semicircular, round-topped arches — never pointed
- Thick, visibly heavy-looking walls
- Small, deeply recessed windows, often splayed wider on the inside
- Squat, block-like towers and generally low rooflines
- Chevron or zigzag carved decoration around openings
- Interior feels dim, enclosed, and weighty rather than light-filled and
  open

## Common Confusion

Visitors and automated classification systems alike very frequently
confuse Romanesque with **Gothic**, since Gothic architecture evolved
directly out of Romanesque and the two styles very often share a single
building site, sometimes even a single structure, where a later Gothic
addition was built onto an earlier Romanesque core. The single fastest way
to tell them apart at a glance remains the arch shape: round means
Romanesque, pointed means Gothic. Romanesque is also occasionally confused
with **Byzantine** architecture, since both traditions favour the round
arch and share a general sense of heavy masonry construction — but
genuine Byzantine buildings typically add a large dome as the dominant
feature, together with rich interior mosaic decoration, neither of which
is characteristic of Romanesque parish churches and cathedrals in Britain.
