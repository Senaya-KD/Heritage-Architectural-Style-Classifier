# Byzantine Architecture: What to Look For

## The Defining Feature: The Dome on a Square Base

The single most important thing to look for in Byzantine architecture is
a large, rounded dome sitting directly above a square or rectangular
building rather than a circular one. This sounds like a simple detail,
but it represents a genuine and historically important engineering
breakthrough — earlier domed buildings could only sit neatly and safely
on a circular plan. Byzantine architects solved the square-to-round
problem using **pendentives**: curved, triangular sections of masonry at
each of the four corners of the square base that smoothly carry the
structural load upward and inward into a continuous circular ring, which
then in turn supports the round dome sitting above it. If you look
upward into the corners beneath a dome and see curved, triangular shapes
filling the space rather than sharp right angles, you are looking at a
pendentive, and you can be confident you are looking at genuine Byzantine
or Byzantine-influenced construction.

## Arches

Like Romanesque architecture, Byzantine buildings consistently use the
semicircular, round arch rather than the pointed arch that would later
define the Gothic style. Byzantine architects frequently used the round
arch to create long, rhythmic arcades of repeated identical openings
running around a central domed space, producing a strong sense of visual
order and repetition.

## Interior Emphasis Over Exterior

Byzantine buildings direct almost all of their architectural and
decorative ambition inward, toward the worshipper standing inside rather
than the passer-by outside. Exteriors are frequently left comparatively
plain — often simply bare, unadorned brick — while interiors are
extraordinarily rich, covered extensively in **mosaic decoration** made
from thousands of small individual pieces of gold-backed glass (called
tesserae), combined with polished marble wall veneers and elaborately
carved stone capitals. This striking contrast between a modest exterior
and a glittering, image-covered interior is one of the most reliable and
memorable ways to identify genuine Byzantine influence in a building.

## Plan and Massing

Byzantine churches are very frequently built on a **centralised plan** —
laid out as a square or a Greek cross shape, organised symmetrically
around the single central domed space — rather than following the long,
processional nave-and-side-aisle arrangement typical of Western Gothic
church design. This gives genuinely Byzantine and Byzantine Revival
buildings a notably compact, gathered, almost self-contained silhouette,
often further emphasised by a cluster of smaller subsidiary domes or
half-domes surrounding and buttressing the main central dome, as seen
clearly at Westminster Cathedral in London.

## Colour and Material

Byzantine architecture makes very extensive and deliberate use of colour.
Interior walls combine marble in several different hues and patterns,
gold mosaic backgrounds behind figurative religious scenes, and extensive
painted or mosaic depictions of saints and biblical narratives covering
large areas of both wall and ceiling surface — a striking contrast with
the comparatively restrained stone carving typical of Western Romanesque
interiors of the same broad period.

## Quick Identification Checklist

- A large, rounded dome positioned above a square or rectangular building
- Visible pendentives — curved triangular sections — at the corners
  beneath the dome
- Round, semicircular arches rather than pointed ones
- A plain or bare-brick exterior contrasted sharply with a richly
  decorated interior
- Gold mosaic decoration, where visible
- A compact, centralised floor plan rather than a long processional nave

## Common Confusion

Byzantine architecture is most often confused with **Romanesque**, since
both traditions rely on the round arch and both use substantial, heavy
masonry construction. The dome is the deciding feature that separates
them: Romanesque churches in Britain are almost always built on a long
rectangular plan without a large central dome dominating the whole
composition, whereas genuinely Byzantine or Byzantine Revival buildings
are organised specifically around one, and this difference in overall
plan and silhouette is usually clear even from a single external
photograph.
