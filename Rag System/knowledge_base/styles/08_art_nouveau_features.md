# Art Nouveau Architecture: What to Look For

## The Defining Feature: Organic, Flowing Curves

Art Nouveau's single clearest and most consistent signature is its
extensive use of long, sinuous, distinctly plant-like curves — often
described as the "whiplash" curve — flowing continuously across
facades, ironwork, and interior details alike. Unlike the sharp
geometric patterning that would later define Art Deco, Art Nouveau
ornament looks as though it grew organically rather than having been
drawn mechanically with a ruler: stems, tendrils, stylised flower forms,
and flowing hair or drapery motifs recur constantly throughout the style,
in materials ranging from carved stone to wrought iron to stained glass.

## Structural Ironwork as Decoration

A particularly distinctive Art Nouveau move is treating structural
ironwork — normally hidden from view or left purely functional in earlier
architecture — as a genuine decorative feature in its own right. Exposed
iron columns, balcony railings, and window frames are deliberately shaped
into elaborate curving plant forms rather than left as the plain,
straight structural bars typical of earlier 19th-century construction.
Hector Guimard's Paris Métro station entrances remain the definitive
textbook example of this particular design approach, and Charles Rennie
Mackintosh's ironwork at the Glasgow School of Art shows a comparable,
though more geometrically restrained, British interpretation of the same
underlying idea.

## Asymmetry

Art Nouveau facades very frequently avoid strict formal symmetry
altogether, instead favouring irregular, flowing overall compositions
that feel considerably closer to natural, organic growth than to the
rigid classical order found in Georgian architecture. Window and door
placement often follows the internal logic of the building's function
rather than an imposed external symmetry.

## Windows

Windows in the Art Nouveau style are frequently unusually shaped —
oval, gently curved, or organically irregular rather than the standard
rectangle used almost universally elsewhere — and may incorporate
stained glass featuring flowing floral or figural patterns, quite unlike
the more rigidly geometric leaded lights typically found in Tudor
Revival work of the same broad period.

## Materials

Art Nouveau architects favoured deliberately combining traditional
building materials, principally stone and brick, with distinctly modern
ones, particularly iron and, somewhat later in the movement, early
reinforced concrete, very often specifically because these newer
materials allowed the flowing, sculptural curves the style demanded in a
way that was considerably harder, and more expensive, to achieve reliably
in solid masonry construction alone.

## Surface Decoration

Facades frequently carry integrated relief sculpture, decorative tile
work, and carved stone ornament depicting stylised plants, flowers, and
occasionally flowing human female figures, blending structure and
applied decoration into a single continuous overall design rather than
treating ornament as something separately applied to the building
afterward, as had been more typical in earlier 19th-century practice.

## Quick Identification Checklist

- Long, sinuous "whiplash" curves used throughout the ornament
- Structural ironwork deliberately shaped decoratively rather than left
  plain
- Asymmetrical, flowing overall composition
- Unusually shaped windows, sometimes featuring flowing stained glass
- Combination of traditional masonry with visibly exposed decorative
  ironwork
- Stylised plant, flower, or figural relief decoration

## Common Confusion

Art Nouveau is most often confused with **Art Deco**, the style that
followed it directly and, in significant part, reacted against it. Both
movements place heavy emphasis on rich surface decoration, and both were
frequently applied to broadly similar building types. The key
distinguishing test remains the underlying character of the curve
itself: Art Nouveau's ornament flows organically, like a growing plant
stem, while Art Deco's ornament is sharp, symmetrical, and distinctly
machine-like in feel. If the decoration looks as though it was drawn by
hand directly from a natural plant form, favour Art Nouveau; if it looks
as though it was constructed from straight lines and precise angles,
favour Art Deco instead.
