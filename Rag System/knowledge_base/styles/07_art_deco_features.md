# Art Deco Architecture: What to Look For

## The Defining Feature: Bold Geometry

Art Deco confidently replaces the flowing curves and natural forms
favoured by earlier decorative styles with sharp, assertive geometric
shapes throughout: zigzags, chevrons, stepped forms, and strong,
insistent rectilinear lines. Decorative motifs are consistently stylised
and deliberately simplified rather than naturalistic — even where a
particular motif is clearly based on a plant or an animal form, it is
rendered as a clean, simplified geometric pattern rather than as anything
approaching a realistic depiction.

## Flat Roofs and Streamlined Forms

Art Deco buildings very frequently feature flat rooflines, marking a
deliberate and visually striking break from the pitched, gabled roofs
typical of earlier domestic and ecclesiastical architectural styles.
Many Art Deco buildings, particularly factories and cinemas, adopt a
distinctly **streamlined** appearance, directly inspired by contemporary
ships, aeroplanes, and automobiles — smooth, gently curved corners
combined with strong horizontal banding running across the facade to
suggest speed and modernity.

## Windows

Continuous horizontal bands of glass, sometimes wrapping smoothly around
building corners without a visible break, are a strong and reliable Art
Deco signal, especially common on factory and commercial buildings of the
period. This is a sharp visual contrast with the individually framed,
separately punched windows typical of earlier Georgian or Tudor Revival
architecture.

## Entrances

Art Deco building entrances are very frequently treated as a dramatic
architectural focal point in their own right, often featuring a stepped
or fan-shaped surround, symmetrical flanking decorative elements, and
bold integrated lettering or signage worked directly into the fabric of
the architecture itself — a clear reflection of the style's close roots
in commercial branding and consumer culture more broadly.

## Materials

Art Deco architects made extensive and enthusiastic use of newly
available or newly fashionable building materials: reinforced concrete,
polished chrome and other bright metals, structural glass block, and
glazed ceramic tile in bold, often deliberately contrasting colours —
cream combined with green being an especially common colour pairing on
English interwar factory buildings specifically.

## Decorative Motifs

Common recurring Art Deco motifs include radiating sunburst patterns,
stepped "ziggurat" forms directly echoing ancient stepped pyramids,
stylised floral shapes reduced to simple, repeated geometric units, and
motifs directly influenced by ancient Egyptian decorative patterning
following the widely publicised 1922 discovery of Tutankhamun's tomb.

## Quick Identification Checklist

- Sharp geometric decoration — zigzags, chevrons, stepped forms
- Flat overall roofline
- Streamlined appearance with curved corners and horizontal banding
- Continuous horizontal bands of windows, sometimes wrapping around
  corners
- Bold, symmetrical entrance featuring integrated signage or lettering
- Cream-and-green, or other boldly contrasting, glazed tile finishes,
  especially common on factory buildings

## Common Confusion

Art Deco is most frequently confused with **Art Nouveau**, the style it
directly followed and consciously reacted against. Both movements place
heavy emphasis on rich decorative surface pattern, and both were very
often applied to broadly similar building types — department stores,
theatres, and hotels in particular — which produces a genuine and
understandable degree of visual overlap between them. The most reliable
distinguishing test is the underlying character of the ornament itself:
Art Nouveau uses flowing, organic, distinctly plant-like curves
throughout, while Art Deco uses sharp, symmetrical, unambiguously
geometric shapes instead. A helpful practical shortcut: if the
decorative pattern looks as though it was constructed with a ruler and a
compass, it is more likely Art Deco; if it looks as though it was drawn
freehand from the curve of a growing plant stem, it is more likely Art
Nouveau.
